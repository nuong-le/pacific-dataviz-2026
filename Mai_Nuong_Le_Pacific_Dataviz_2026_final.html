01 · Right now

Is the Pacific seeing more disasters — or facing a higher baseline of risk?

The recent picture is anything but simple. In 2025, the wider Pacific typhoon basin recorded 27 named storms, including 13 typhoons, linked to 653 deaths and about US$10.8 billion in damage. Yet the 2025–26 South Pacific cyclone season, covering countries such as Fiji, Tonga, Vanuatu and Samoa, produced only 2 cyclones — a record low, tied with 1990–91.

"So, is climate change making Pacific disasters more frequent?"
Storm counts can swing sharply from year to year because of natural climate variability such as La Niña. That means one quiet cyclone season does not tell us whether the underlying climate risk is changing. The more consistent signal in this analysis is the baseline underneath those events: temperatures have risen over the long term, while sea level has also continued to rise. A storm, king tide or heavy rainfall event now occurs against that changing background. Earthquakes and tsunamis are different — they are geological hazards — but for low-lying Pacific communities they can add to the same underlying exposure.

Storm statistics: 2025 Pacific typhoon season and 2025–26 South Pacific cyclone season records. Elevation exposure: small island developing states (SIDS) literature. See references in the footer.

02 · Method

There are many signals. Which ones move most closely with the warming?

Rather than throwing every indicator at the story, we let the data pick: a technique called forward selection adds one predictor at a time, keeping whichever most improves the model, and stops once the rest stop pulling their weight.

Sea-level anomalies were selected first. Adding greenhouse-gas emissions, rainfall anomalies and crop yield produced a final model with an adjusted R² of 0.64. These four indicators showed the strongest combined relationship with regional temperature in the selected model.

This is an exploratory, correlational model based on 28 annual observations. It identifies patterns that move together; it does not prove that one indicator causes another.

04 · Sea level

Every island. Rising water.

Sea level is one of the clearest and most consistent signals in the dataset. 21 of 22 territories have sea-level data, and all 21 show a rising trend (Pitcairn has no sea-level records), with most estimates falling within a relatively narrow range.

R² 0.75 · p<0.001 — strong and consistent regional trend

The direction is remarkably consistent. That makes elevation and exposure especially important: low-lying atoll communities have less physical room to absorb the same change in sea level.

Falling emissions. Rising heat.

The correlation shows the contrast, but it does not establish a causal relationship.

The relationships are not equally clear across every indicator. Crop yield showed no statistically significant regional trend in this analysis (p=0.40). That is important too: climate signals are not uniform, and the absence of a clear trend should not be turned into a stronger claim than the data supports.

Four climate action priorities.

01 · Protect
Prioritise the most exposed
Direct adaptation toward low-elevation communities where sea-level rise and coastal hazards can translate into disproportionate impacts.
02 · Transition
Build resilient clean energy
Build on the region's growing renewable-energy capacity and pair cleaner generation with resilient, decentralised systems.
03 · Finance
Close the emissions–impact gap
Keep climate finance and regional diplomacy focused on adaptation needs in communities contributing relatively little to global emissions.
04 · Monitor
Turn indicators into early warning
Track rainfall, livestock and land-cover changes alongside climate indicators to identify emerging pressure and support earlier action.
→Policy mechanism: environmental taxes and incentives can support the transition where they fit local economies.

The Pacific is not starting from zero.

Across climate policy, Pacific resilience and renewable-energy planning, many of these priorities are already being pursued. The challenge is turning them into action at the scale and speed the data suggests is needed.

Data & method
Show sources and analysis notes

Source: Pacific Community (SPC) Climate Change Indicators dataset — 16,568 observations covering 22 Pacific Island countries and territories and 13 indicators.

Cleaning: coverage varies substantially between indicators and territories. Country-name variants were standardised, incomplete observations were removed, and available observations were aggregated by year to create regional mean series. The modelling period was then restricted to years with sufficient coverage across the selected candidate variables.

Trend analysis: regional indicator series were analysed using ordinary least-squares regression against year. Linear and quadratic specifications were compared using AIC. Country-level regressions were also used to check whether the main temperature and sea-level patterns were visible beyond the regional average.

Feature selection: forward selection was applied to a 1995–2022 series (n=28) using 7 candidate indicators with regional surface temperature anomaly as the target. At each step, the remaining variable that produced the largest improvement in adjusted R² was added. Selection stopped when the improvement from another variable was less than 0.005. The four selected indicators were, in order: Sea Level Anomalies, Greenhouse Gas Emissions per Capita, Precipitation Anomalies and Crop Yield. The final adjusted R² was 0.64.

Variables not used in the final model: not every available indicator was suitable for the final climate model. Some had limited coverage, while others — including monitoring-network and fisheries-management measures — can reflect changes in reporting, governance or infrastructure rather than a direct climate signal. These variables were therefore not treated as climate drivers simply because they showed statistical association.

Forecast: linear and quadratic trend models were compared using AIC. Sea level was retained as a linear trend, while temperature showed a substantially better quadratic fit (R² 0.76 versus 0.39). These are statistical extrapolations from historical data, not climate-model scenarios. Uncertainty increases as the projection moves further beyond the observed period.
